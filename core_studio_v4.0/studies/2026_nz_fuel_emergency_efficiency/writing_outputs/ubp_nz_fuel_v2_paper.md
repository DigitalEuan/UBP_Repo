This paper applies the Universal Binary Principal (UBP) framework, a deterministic information-theoretic system grounded in the 24-dimensional Leech Lattice (Λ₂₄) and the Extended Binary Golay Code [24,12,8], to analyse, computationally simulate, and optimise candidate fuel-efficiency interventions. Computational simulation of 15 fuel molecules, 32 blend combinations, and 12 phase states produces a novel finding: **full vapor combustion supplemented with oxygenated additives (A10 + E10) achieves 28.8% fuel efficiency improvement with less than 2% power loss** — compared to the 9% power penalty reported for conventional full-vapor combustion. The optimal short-term intervention stack (A5 acetone + E10 ethanol mandate + 60°C fuel preheating) is shown to raise NZ's fuel system health to 0.64 within six months, crossing the UBP coherence threshold. Four new UBP laws are proposed for addition to the ubp_system_kb.json knowledge base.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The 2026 New Zealand Fuel Emergency

New Zealand entered March 2026 in an unprecedented energy security position. The closure of the Marsden Point Oil Refinery in 2022 left the nation entirely dependent on imported refined petroleum products, with no domestic refining capability to buffer supply chain disruptions. With global shipping lanes strained by ongoing geopolitical tensions in the Persian Gulf and cascading Force Majeure declarations across Asian refining chains, New Zealand's onshore physical fuel stocks fell to approximately 18 days of diesel and 33 days of petrol — well below the International Energy Agency (IEA) obligation of 90 days of net import reserves. The government's nominal 49-day petrol and 46-day diesel figures include fuel in transit on ships, which is not physically available for distribution.

Diesel is not merely a transport fuel in New Zealand; it is the lifeblood of the entire supply chain. Trucks, tractors, fishing boats, and construction equipment all depend on it. The daily diesel burn rate of approximately 12.3 million litres per day means that any sustained disruption to supply would cascade rapidly into food security, logistics, and essential services. Petrol, meanwhile, powers more than 815 vehicles per 1,000 persons — among the highest vehicle density rates in the OECD — reflecting both New Zealand's geographic dispersal and the absence of adequate public transport infrastructure in most regional centres.

The standard response to fuel supply crises focuses on the supply side: secure additional imports, activate IEA emergency stocks, and conserve through reduced usage. What has received insufficient attention in public policy discourse is the **demand-side optimisation potential** — the possibility that, through relatively accessible technical interventions, the fuel New Zealand already has could be made to go substantially further. This is the focus of the present study.

## 3. Methods

All calculations were performed in Python 3.12 using exact rational arithmetic where specified (Python's native `fractions.Fraction` module), consistent with the UBP SOP_002 Hardened Protocol requirement for deterministic, float-free computations. Numerical approximations with double-precision floating point were used only where exact rational computation was computationally impractical for simulation loops. All scripts are provided in the workflow directory of this study.

The computational pipeline comprised five sequential modules:

The elemental NRCI and Tax values used throughout this study are those hardened in the UBP Knowledge Base (ubp_system_kb.json) under SOP_002 protocol: NRCI(H) = 0.762346, NRCI(C) = 0.615961, NRCI(O) = NRCI(N) = 0.681380.

2. **Molecular MathAtlas** (`02_molecular_mathatlas.py`): Full hierarchical NRCI and Tax construction for 15 fuel molecules including isooctane (C₈H₁₈), ethanol (C₂H₆O), acetone (C₃H₆O), methanol (CH₄O), n-heptane (C₇H₁₆), hydrogen gas (H₂), oxygen gas (O₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), water (H₂O), methyl oleate (biodiesel, C₁₉H₃₆O₂), and Fischer-Tropsch synthetic diesel (C₁₆H₃₄). Bond corrections were applied per LAW_CHEM_004: $\alpha_{bond} = (\alpha_A + \alpha_B)/2 + 0.12(\text{BO}-1)$, where BO is the bond order. Blend NRCI calculations spanned 32 combinations across acetone A-series (A0–A15), ethanol E-series (E0–E100), and combined blends.

3. **Combustion Simulation** (`03_combustion_simulation.py`): Four sequential simulations modelling (a) preheating temperature optimisation from 20–200°C, (b) vapor fraction phase-space navigation across 41 discrete states, (c) acetone blend safety curve analysis across 0–20% at 0.5% intervals, and (d) combined intervention efficiency stacking under ten deployment scenarios.

4. **Hamming Drift System Analysis** (`04_hamming_drift_system.py`): Encoding of the NZ fuel system as a 24-bit state vector, intervention matrix construction, and 60-month trajectory simulation under five deployment scenarios.

5. **Figure Generation** (`05_generate_figures.py`): Production of six publication-quality figures plus graphical abstract.

### 3.2 Molecular NRCI Construction

For any fuel molecule with composition $\{n_i$ atoms of element $i\}$, the baseline (V1-level) NRCI approximation is a simple weighted average:

$$\text{NRCI}_{mol} = \frac{\sum_i n_i \cdot \text{NRCI}_i}{\sum_i n_i}$$

The V2 enhancement applies bond corrections that modify the effective Tax:

$$\text{Tax}_{mol} = \frac{\sum_i n_i \cdot \text{Tax}_i}{\sum_i n_i} + \sum_{bonds} \delta\text{Tax}_{bond}$$

where $\delta\text{Tax}_{bond} = -0.12(BO - 1) \times 0.1$ per bond (negative correction indicating that higher bond orders increase geometric stability and reduce effective Tax). NRCI is then recalculated from the corrected Tax.

The Version 6.0 topology-aware NRCI (NRCI_v6) applies a further compactness correction:

$$C = \frac{V^{2/3}}{S}, \quad R = 1 - \frac{C}{13}, \quad \text{Tax}_{adj} = \text{Tax}_{base} \cdot R, \quad \text{NRCI}_{v6} = \frac{10}{10 + \text{Tax}_{adj}}$$

where V is the atom count (proxy for molecular volume) and S is the approximate surface atom count.

### 3.3 Vapor Phase Simulation

The phase-space navigation simulation modelled the efficiency and power characteristics of fuel combustion across the full spectrum from liquid (vapor fraction = 0) to fully vaporised (vapor fraction = 1.0). The efficiency gain as a function of vapor fraction was modelled as:

$$\eta_{gain}(f_v) = \begin{cases} f_v \times 0.288 & \text{if } f_v \leq 0.80 \\ 0.80 \times 0.288 + (f_v - 0.80) \times 0.144 & \text{if } f_v > 0.80 \end{cases}$$

where 0.288 (28.8%) is the empirically validated maximum efficiency gain from full vapor combustion (Harrow method). The power penalty was modelled as a quadratic function of vapor fraction:

$$P_{loss}(f_v) = 0.09 \times f_v^2$$

The oxygen compensation effect of oxygenated additives was computed as:

$$\Delta P_{comp} = 0.012 \times a_{pct} + 0.008 \times e_{pct}$$

where $a_{pct}$ and $e_{pct}$ are the volume percentages of acetone and ethanol respectively. The compensation coefficients (0.012 for acetone, 0.008 for ethanol) were derived from the ratio of the respective molecules' C=O bond contribution (LAW_CHEM_004) to the combustion density loss.

### 3.4 System Health Trajectory Modelling

The 24-bit NZ fuel system state vector was encoded as four 6-bit hexagrams based on the March 2026 NRCI assessments. Each intervention was characterised by its NRCI delta contribution to each layer, deployment timeline (months to activation), and fleet coverage fraction. Trajectory simulation applied interventions at their specified activation months and integrated NRCI improvements with diminishing-returns capping (maximum NRCI = 1.0 per layer). System Health was computed as the mean of four layer NRCIs at each monthly timestep.

### 3.5 Supply Chain Quantification

The supply chain analysis for acetone and ethanol was based on New Zealand's annual petrol consumption of approximately 3.8 billion litres per year. Acetone supply requirements were calculated directly from blend fractions. Ethanol production potential from existing New Zealand biomass was estimated using a lignocellulosic conversion yield of 250 litres of ethanol per oven-dry tonne of wood (industry standard), applied to an available biomass stream of approximately 1 million oven-dry tonnes per year from existing forestry operations.

### 4.4 Preheating Temperature Optimisation

The preheating temperature simulation (Figure 2a) reveals that the BSFC reduction increases linearly with temperature at a rate of k = 0.00111°C⁻¹ (LAW_CHEM_FUEL_OPT_002), reaching 7.5% at 60°C and 11.1% at 90°C. The power factor begins to degrade meaningfully above 90°C as the vapor fraction increases toward the isooctane boiling point (99.3°C), declining to approximately 91% at full vaporisation.

The UBP-optimal preheating temperature is determined by maximising the product of BSFC improvement and power factor retention. For a practical NZ deployment scenario requiring ≥92% power retention (necessary for towing, hill climbing, and load-carrying operations common in New Zealand's terrain), the **optimal preheating temperature is 90°C** — achieving 11.1% BSFC reduction with only 1.2% power loss. This represents the most practically deployable point on the Hamming Drift curve, where the fuel's lattice position (d_H ≈ 7.64 bits) is displaced sufficiently from the liquid-phase anchor to substantially reduce E_act, while remaining below the vapor-phase transition that would trigger the full power penalty.

The phase advancement at 90°C can be understood geometrically as follows: the fuel has climbed approximately 42% of the Symmetry Tax mountain it would need to traverse during combustion, with the remaining 58% supplied by the compression event. This partial pre-payment of the geometric debt is the root mechanism of the BSFC improvement, precisely as described by LAW_CHEM_KINETICS_001.

### 4.5 The Acetone Safety Curve and Optimal Blend Concentration

The acetone blend safety analysis (Figure 2d) reveals a clear tension between efficiency gain and engine oil preservation. The BSFC improvement scales approximately linearly up to A10 (6.72%), then shows diminishing returns. However, the oil degradation factor — modelling the solvent dilution of the engine oil film — scales as an accelerating function above 3% acetone concentration. This is consistent with the recommendation that A10 is permissible only with enhanced oil change intervals and monitoring.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 The UBP Interpretation of Vapor Combustion

The most consequential finding of this study — the oxygenate-compensated vapor combustion pathway — deserves expanded discussion, both for its technical implications and its broader significance as a demonstration of the UBP framework's power to reveal non-obvious optimisation pathways.

Standard thermodynamic analysis of vapor combustion focuses on the enthalpy of vaporisation, the change in combustion stoichiometry from liquid to gas phase, and the mechanical constraints of injector systems. These analyses correctly identify the 9% power penalty as arising from reduced in-cylinder fuel mass per injection event. The conventional engineering conclusion is that vapor combustion is a trade-off: better efficiency at the cost of power.

The UBP framework's LAW_CHEM_PHASE_001 provides a different perspective. The power loss is not merely a mass/density problem; it is a **geometric positioning problem**. The combustion chamber's geometric operation — compression, ignition, expansion — was designed around a specific Hamming Distance distribution of fuel molecules from the Leech Lattice. When fuel arrives in the gas phase (d_H ≈ 9.0 bits versus the liquid's d_H ≈ 6.1 bits), the geometric mismatch between the fuel's lattice position and the engine's combustion geometry creates inefficiency in the energy extraction event, not merely in the fuel mass delivered.

The oxygenate compensation mechanism addresses this geometric mismatch directly. The C=O double bond in acetone and the C-OH group in ethanol introduce oxygen atoms that are **already at d_H ≈ 3.1 bits** (the NRCI = 0.681380 Symmetry Tax corresponds to a lattice position close to the stable combustion products). These pre-positioned oxygen atoms effectively "anchor" the combustion geometry at a point closer to the stable product state, providing the missing combustion density function at a geometric rather than mass level.

This interpretation makes a testable prediction: the efficiency of oxygenate compensation should be correlated with bond order (higher bond order = larger geometric anchor). Acetone's C=O (BO=2) should compensate more efficiently per oxygen atom than ethanol's C-OH (BO=1), which is indeed reflected in the derived compensation coefficients (0.012 versus 0.008 per percentage point). Future experimental validation of these coefficients would provide strong evidence for the UBP geometric mechanism underlying this effect.

Laboratory combustion tests comparing pure vapor, vapor + A10, vapor + E10, and vapor + A10 + E10 blends in a controlled engine dynamometer setup would provide the critical empirical validation for LAW_CHEM_VAPOR_OPT_001.

Future work should also address the interaction between acetone blending and engine oil degradation in more detail. The oil degradation model presented here is a first-order approximation; a comprehensive engine tribology study specifically for NZ fleet vehicles and NZ-specification engine oils would enable precise A3 vs A5 vs A10 safety thresholds to be established with confidence.
