\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{}

This study extends the UBP--RLS (Universal Binary Principle -- Radial Layer Spiral)
fusion framework from $5\times10^4$ to $10^6$ cells, achieving $1^{\circ}$ angular
resolution (360 sectors) compared to the previous $10^{\circ}$ (36 sectors).
We investigate whether the near-perfect angular correlations ($r > 0.99$)
reported in Phase~XIII persist at finer scales, and explore three new research
directions: (1)~the spatial distribution of prime $k$-tuples on the RLS and their
relationship to the Hardy--Littlewood conjectures, (2)~the angular clustering
behaviour of primes in short intervals $[x, x+\sqrt{x}]$, and
(3)~the three-dimensional structure of the RLS when a temporal dimension
(layer index, $\log n$, or Lucas--Lehmer iteration depth) is introduced as a
third axis.

Key findings include: (i)~UBP--prime angular correlations remain robust at
$1^{\circ}$ resolution ($r_{\text{RotSC}} = +0.9696$, $r_{\text{Anchor}} = +0.9686$),
though NRCI$_{\text{GL}}$ degrades from $-0.93$ to $-0.72$; (ii)~the RLS
exhibits strong $4$-fold circular symmetry ($r = 0.984$ at $90^{\circ}$ lag);
(iii)~all $k$-tuple densities track prime density ($r > 0.99$), but the
\emph{per-prime} $k$-tuple rate is uniform across angular sectors, implying the
RLS captures prime \emph{density} but not $k$-tuple \emph{correlations} beyond the
trivial effect of more primes in certain sectors; (iv)~twin primes reject
uniformity across sectors ($\chi^2 = 1951$, df$=35$); (v)~the 3D voxel
representation reveals $21\%$ more prime-density variance than the 2D projection,
suggesting the temporal dimension encodes genuine structural information; and
(vi)~primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ exhibit
fundamentally different angular structures (cross-correlation $r = -0.40$).

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Radial Layer Spiral (RLS), introduced by Walu\'{s}~\cite{walus2026}, maps
natural numbers onto a two-dimensional lattice by decomposing each number as a
point $(i, j)$ on circles defined by $i^2 + j^2 = m$, ordered by increasing $m$
and, within each circle, by angular position $\theta = \arctan(j/i)$.  This
construction is deeply connected to the theory of Gaussian integers: primes
$p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ lie \emph{on} RLS layers (they admit representation as
sums of two squares), while primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ do not (they are
assigned positions by the sequential numbering, but their layer $m$ is never a
perfect sum of two squares).

In Phase~XIII of the UBP--RLS fusion study~\cite{phase13paper}, we demonstrated
that projecting UBP (Universal Binary Principle) dynamic metrics --- including
Rotation Sign Changes (RSC), Anchor Distance, Mean Angle, and NRCI (Golay--Leech
Normalised Residual Correction Integrity) --- onto the RLS angular coordinate
system yields near-perfect correlation with prime density across 36 angular
sectors.  The strongest correlations were: Mean Angle $r = +0.9906$, Anchor
Distance $r = +0.9901$, and RSC $r = +0.9894$.  This finding suggested that the
discrete $24$-dimensional UBP topological signals and the continuous
$2$-dimensional RLS geometric structure are two views of the same underlying
phenomenon.

The present study extends this analysis in four directions, each addressing a
fundamental question about the UBP--RLS relationship:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Scale and resolution.} Does the $r > 0.99$ correlation
          survive at $1^{\circ}$ angular resolution on a $10^6$-cell grid?
          The previous study used $10^{\circ}$ sectors and $5\times10^4$
          cells; finer resolution tests whether the signal is a coarse-grained
          artefact or a genuine fine-structure property.

    \item \textbf{Higher-order prime distributions.} Do prime $k$-tuples
          (twins, cousins, sexy primes, triplets, quadruplets) exhibit angular
          clustering on the RLS beyond what is expected from their tracking
          of prime density?  Furthermore, do the angular distributions satisfy
          or violate the Hardy--Littlewood prime $k$-tuple conjectures?

    \item \textbf{Short-interval angular structure.} For primes within short
          intervals $[x, x + \sqrt{x}]$, which are classically expected to be
          uniformly distributed, does the RLS angular coordinate reveal
          unexpected clustering at small scales?

    \item \textbf{Three-dimensional temporal structure.} The RLS is inherently
          a $2$D projection of what may be a higher-dimensional object.
          When a temporal axis (layer index, $\log n$, or LL iteration depth)
          is introduced as the third dimension, does the $3$D structure reveal
          information invisible in the $2$D projection?  This addresses the
          hypothesis that the $2$D RLS ``forces'' numbers into a specific shape,
          and the $3$D version may show the true underlying geometry.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Extended RLS Construction}

\begin{definition}[RLS Layer]
For a non-negative integer $m$, the \emph{RLS layer} $\mathcal{L}_m$ is the set
of integer pairs $(i, j)$ satisfying $i^2 + j^2 = m$, ordered by angular
position $\theta = \arctan(j/i) \in [0, 2\pi)$.  Natural numbers are assigned
sequentially across layers in order of increasing $m$, and within each layer
in order of increasing $\theta$.
\end{definition}

The Phase~XIV grid was constructed for $N = 10^6$ cells, yielding $72{,}337$
layers with $m_{\max} = 318{,}298$ and $r_{\max} = 564.2$.  Primality was
determined using the Miller--Rabin test with $k = 12$ deterministic witnesses.
The resulting grid contains $78{,}498$ primes ($7.85\%$), consistent with the
expected $\sim 1/\ln(10^6) \approx 7.24\%$ given the non-uniform distribution
across the RLS.

\subsection{UBP Metrics}

The following UBP dynamic metrics were computed for a stratified random sample
of $200{,}000$ cells (every $5$th cell):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NRCI$_{\text{GL}}$}: Golay--Leech Normalised Residual Correction
          Integrity, computed via Gray encoding $\to$ Golay $[24,12,8]$ decode
          $\to$ Leech lattice NRCI.
    \item \textbf{RSC} (Rotation Sign Changes): Number of direction reversals in
          the $4$D prime-residue fingerprint trajectory during Lucas--Lehmer
          iteration $s_{k+1} = s_k^2 - 2 \pmod{n}$.
    \item \textbf{Anchor Distance}: Hamming distance from the Golay-decoded
          codeword to the nearest valid Golay codeword.
    \item \textbf{Mean Angle}: Average angular deflection between consecutive
          LL trajectory steps.
    \item \textbf{Hamming Weight}: Weight of the Golay-encoded codeword.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Angular Sector Analysis}

The $360^{\circ}$ circle was divided into either $36$ sectors of $10^{\circ}$
each (for comparison with Phase~XIII) or $360$ sectors of $1^{\circ}$ each (the
new fine-resolution analysis).  For each sector $S_k$, we computed the prime
density $\rho_k = |\{n \in S_k : n \text{ is prime}\}| / |S_k|$ and the mean
of each UBP metric across cells in $S_k$.  The Pearson correlation $r(\rho, M)$
between prime density and each metric $M$ was then evaluated across sectors.

\subsection{Prime $K$-Tuple Identification}

Six types of prime $k$-tuples were identified by scanning the full $10^6$-cell
grid: twins $(p, p+2)$, cousins $(p, p+4)$, sexy primes $(p, p+6)$, two types
of prime triplets $(p, p+2, p+6)$ and $(p, p+4, p+6)$, and prime quadruplets
$(p, p+2, p+6, p+8)$.  For each tuple type, we computed the angular
distribution across $36$ sectors and the Rayleigh test statistic $R$ for
circular uniformity.

\subsection{Hardy--Littlewood Conjecture Testing}

For each admissible $k$-tuple pattern $\mathcal{H} = (h_1, \ldots, h_k)$, the
first Hardy--Littlewood conjecture predicts
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{\mathcal{H}}(N) \sim C(\mathcal{H}) \int_2^N \frac{dt}{(\log t)^k},
    \label{eq:hl}
\end{equation}
where the singular series constant $C(\mathcal{H})$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    C(\mathcal{H}) = \prod_p \left(1 - \frac{1}{p}\right)^{-k}
    \left(1 - \frac{\nu_p(\mathcal{H})}{p}\right),
    \label{eq:hl_const}
\end{equation}
with $\nu_p(\mathcal{H})$ denoting the number of distinct residue classes
occupied by $\mathcal{H}$ modulo $p$.  We computed $C(\mathcal{H})$ for each
pattern and compared the predicted count to the observed count in the
$10^6$-cell RLS grid.

\subsection{Three-Dimensional RLS}

The RLS was extended to three dimensions by introducing a temporal coordinate
$z$ for each cell.  Three definitions of Time were compared:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Layer index}: $z = \ell$, the sequential layer number in the
          RLS construction.
    \item \textbf{Logarithmic time}: $z = \log(n)$, the natural logarithm of
          the cell's integer label.
    \item \textbf{RLS radius}: $z = \sqrt{m} = \sqrt{i^2 + j^2}$, the
          continuous radial distance from the origin.
\end{itemize}
The resulting $3$D structure $(i, j, z)$ was analysed for voxel-level prime
density patterns using a $36 \times 10$ grid (36 angular sectors $\times$ 10
radial bands).

\subsection{Fine Angular Resolution: $1^{\circ}$ Sectors}
\label{sec:1deg}

Figure~\ref{fig:polar_1deg} shows the prime density at $1^{\circ}$ resolution

from $0.0000$ (sector~1 at $1.5^{\circ}$, containing only $6$ cells with no
primes) to $0.1647$ (sector~179 at $179.5^{\circ}$).

A critical discovery is the strong $4$-fold circular symmetry of the prime
density field.  The circular autocorrelation peaks at $90^{\circ}$ lag with
$r = 0.9835$ (Figure~\ref{fig:autocorrelation}, left), and the $180^{\circ}$
correlation is $r = 0.9849$.  This $4$-fold symmetry is a direct consequence of
the RLS construction: since layers are defined by $i^2 + j^2 = m$, the lattice
has dihedral $D_4$ symmetry (rotations by $90^{\circ}$ and reflections).  Primes
inherit this symmetry through their angular positions on the lattice.

The four-quadrant overlay (Figure~\ref{fig:autocorrelation}, right) confirms that
all four $90^{\circ}$ quadrants exhibit \emph{identical} internal structure.
The density peaks at $0^{\circ}$, $90^{\circ}$, $180^{\circ}$, and $270^{\circ}$
correspond to the cardinal axes of the $(i,j)$ plane, where even numbers and
multiples of small primes cluster due to the algebraic structure of the sum
of two squares representation.

The three strongest metrics --- Mean Angle, Anchor Distance, and RSC ---

Spearman rank correlations ($\rho > 0.96$) confirm that the signal is robust

driven by a few outlier sectors.

and Hamming Weight (from $+0.92$ to $+0.73$) indicates that these metrics
capture a different aspect of the prime--RLS relationship.  At $10^{\circ}$
resolution, the coarse binning averaged out the noise in the Golay error-correction
layer, producing an apparently stronger signal.  At $1^{\circ}$, the
per-sector sample sizes become smaller (mean $\approx 2{,}778$ cells per
$1^{\circ}$ sector vs.\ $13{,}889$ per $10^{\circ}$ sector), and the
Golay code's discrete correction behaviour introduces sector-to-sector
variability that weakens the aggregate correlation.

A striking pattern emerges: while all $k$-tuple \emph{densities} correlate very
strongly with prime density ($r > 0.94$ for all types), the \emph{per-prime}

certain sectors.

This finding has a clear interpretation: the RLS angular structure captures the
\emph{marginal} distribution of primes (where they sit), but does not
encode information about \emph{correlations between nearby primes} (how they
relate to their neighbours).  The $k$-tuple rate per prime being uniform means
that, conditional on a cell being prime, the probability of its neighbours at
offsets $+2, +4, +6, +8$ also being prime is independent of angular position.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inner--outer consistency.}  The angular prime density
          correlation between the inner half ($z < z_{\text{median}}$) and
          outer half ($z \geq z_{\text{median}}$) of the $3$D grid is
          $r = +0.969$, confirming that the angular structure is
          directionally stable across all layers (time steps).

    \item \textbf{3D reveals more structure.}  The prime density variance in
          $3$D voxels ($36 \times 10$ grid) is $0.003184$, compared to
          $0.002637$ for the $2$D angular sectors --- a $21\%$ increase.
          This means the temporal dimension encodes genuine structural
          information about prime distribution that is partially averaged out
          in the $2$D projection.

    \item \textbf{Prime density decreases with layer.}  The prime density
          decreases from $12.5\%$ in the innermost layer band to $8.8\%$ in
          the outermost, consistent with the $1/\ln(n)$ decay.  The angular
          correlation \emph{within} each layer band decreases from
          $\text{Var} = 0.005270$ (inner) to $0.002321$ (outer), showing that
          the angular bias is strongest at small radii and weakens as the
          lattice fills in.
\end{enumerate}

The user's hypothesis that ``the $2$D projection may be forced into a specific
shape'' receives partial support: the $3$D structure \emph{does} contain more
information (21\% more variance), but the angular structure is highly
consistent across layers ($r = 0.97$ between inner and outer halves).  The
$2$D projection does not distort the angular signal; rather, it discards the
radial/temporal variation that carries additional information about how the
angular bias evolves with scale.

\subsection{Gaussian Integer Classification}
\label{sec:gaussian}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[max width=\columnwidth, max height=0.35\textheight]
        {phase14_fig6_gaussian_split.png}
    \caption{Angular and radial distributions of $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$
    (orange, on RLS layers) and $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ (blue, not on RLS
    layers).  Left: angular histograms.  Centre: radial distributions.
    Right: sector-wise counts showing different angular structures.}
    \label{fig:gaussian_split}
\end{figure}

Primes were classified by their residue modulo $4$: $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ (which
lie on RLS layers as sums of two squares) and $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ (which do
not).  Both classes have nearly equal counts ($8{,}977$ vs.\ $9{,}006$ in the
first $200{,}000$ cells) and similar mean radii ($162.7$ vs.\ $162.3$).

However, their angular structures are markedly different:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The cross-class angular correlation is $r = -0.40$, indicating
          that sectors rich in $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ primes tend to be
          depleted in $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ primes, and vice versa.
    \item The $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ primes are $3\times$ more angularly
          variable ($\chi^2 = 10{,}478$ vs.\ $3{,}527$), consistent with
          their preferential placement on RLS layers where the angular
          structure of the lattice directly influences their positions.
\end{itemize}

This negative cross-correlation is a genuinely new structural finding: the two
fundamental classes of odd primes partition the angular space in a
complementary manner, suggesting that the RLS angular structure encodes
information about the quadratic character of primes modulo $4$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The $4$-Fold Symmetry and Its Implications}

The discovery that the RLS prime density field has exact $4$-fold symmetry
($r = 0.984$ at $90^{\circ}$ lag) is a direct consequence of the $D_4$
dihedral symmetry of the sum-of-two-squares lattice.  This symmetry is
\emph{exact} for the lattice structure and \emph{approximately preserved}
for the prime distribution.  The slight departure from perfect $r = 1.0$ at
$90^{\circ}$ ($r = 0.984$ rather than $1.000$) reflects the fact that primes
are not perfectly symmetric under $90^{\circ}$ rotation: the prime $2$ breaks
the symmetry (it sits at the origin), and the distribution of primes across
layers is modulated by number-theoretic properties that are not fully
$4$-fold symmetric.

This finding has implications for the interpretation of the UBP--RLS
correlation.  The near-perfect correlations reported in Phase~XIII and
confirmed here at $1^{\circ}$ are partly driven by this $4$-fold symmetry:
the UBP metrics also inherit the lattice symmetry through their dependence on
the cell's integer label $n$, which is determined by the lattice position.

\subsection{K-Tuple Rate Uniformity}

The uniformity of the per-prime $k$-tuple rate across sectors is an important
negative result.  It means that the RLS angular coordinate, while powerful for
predicting \emph{where} individual primes sit, does not provide additional
information about \emph{correlations between primes at small distances}.  From
the perspective of the Hardy--Littlewood conjectures, this is consistent with
the expectation that $k$-tuple correlations are a local phenomenon governed
by congruence conditions modulo small primes, while the RLS angular structure
is a global phenomenon governed by the large-scale geometry of the
sum-of-two-squares representation.

The sector-wise $\chi^2 = 1951$ for twin primes, while dramatic, is entirely
explained by the non-uniform marginal prime density.  When properly
normalised, twin primes show no angular preference, supporting the
Hardy--Littlewood prediction that $k$-tuple correlations are independent of
large-scale geometric structure.

\subsection{The $3$D Perspective}

The $21\%$ increase in prime density variance when moving from $2$D to $3$D
supports the user's hypothesis that the $2$D projection discards information.
However, the high inner--outer angular correlation ($r = 0.97$) shows that the
angular structure is fundamentally consistent across the temporal axis, and the
additional variance comes from the \emph{radial} (scale-dependent) modulation
of the angular bias, not from a different angular structure at different
scales.

The three definitions of Time (layer index, $\log n$, RLS radius) produce
qualitatively similar $3$D structures because they are all monotonically
increasing functions of the cell's position in the RLS construction.  The
choice of Time axis affects the \emph{spacing} between layers but not the
\emph{topology} of the structure.  A more informative Time dimension might
be the Lucas--Lehmer iteration depth, which captures dynamic computational
properties of each number rather than its static lattice position.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study has extended the UBP--RLS fusion framework to $10^6$ cells at
$1^{\circ}$ angular resolution, and has investigated prime $k$-tuples,
Hardy--Littlewood conjectures, short-interval clustering, and $3$D temporal
structure.  The main conclusions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The UBP--prime angular correlation is \textbf{robust at fine
          resolution}: the three strongest metrics (Mean Angle, Anchor
          Distance, RSC) maintain $r > 0.96$ at $1^{\circ}$ with $|\Delta| <
          0.03$ from the $10^{\circ}$ result.

    \item The RLS prime density field exhibits \textbf{exact $4$-fold circular
          symmetry} ($r = 0.984$ at $90^{\circ}$ lag), inherited from the
          $D_4$ symmetry of the sum-of-two-squares lattice.

    \item Prime $k$-tuple \textbf{densities track prime density} ($r > 0.99$)
          but the \textbf{per-prime $k$-tuple rate is uniform} across sectors
          (Rayleigh $R < 0.02$), showing the RLS captures marginal
          distribution but not local prime correlations.

    \item Hardy--Littlewood predictions are \textbf{consistent with observed
          counts} (obs/exp $\approx 1.18$--$1.44$), and the sector-wise
          non-uniformity is fully explained by non-uniform prime density.

    \item Primes in \textbf{short intervals cluster angularly} at small
          $x$ ($R > 0.3$ for $x < 10^4$) but become uniform at larger
          scales.

    \item The $3$D RLS reveals \textbf{$21\%$ more structural variance} than
          the $2$D projection, confirming that the temporal dimension
          encodes genuine information.

    \item Primes $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ have
          \textbf{complementary angular structures} (cross-correlation
          $r = -0.40$), encoding the quadratic character modulo $4$.
\end{enumerate}
