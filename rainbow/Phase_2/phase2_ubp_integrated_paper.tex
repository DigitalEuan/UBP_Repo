\section{Introduction}

The rainbow, a quintessential optical phenomenon, has been the subject of scientific inquiry for centuries, from Descartes' geometric optics explanation [1] to Airy's wave theory treatment of supernumerary arcs [2]. The primary and secondary rainbows, appearing at approximately 42° and 51° respectively, are well understood. However, the existence and structure of higher-order rainbows remain a frontier of optical physics. While the tertiary (p=3) and quaternary (p=4) rainbows have been photographed in nature only recently [3, 4], laboratory experiments have confirmed the existence of rainbows up to the 200th order [5].

A prior investigation (Phase 1) by the authors established a novel connection between the 42° primary rainbow and the 116.565° dihedral angle of the dodecahedron, a core geometric constraint in the Universal Binary Principle (UBP) framework [6]. It also uncovered a golden ratio (φ) relationship for the secondary rainbow, with an angular separation of approximately 6φ from the primary. This raised a critical question: does this geometric structure persist for higher orders?

This paper addresses that question through a comprehensive Phase 2 investigation utilizing the full capabilities of the UBP 3.4 framework. We present the first complete calculation of all 200 observable rainbow orders and analyze the resulting patterns. Our findings reveal a rich, quantized geometric structure far more complex and elegant than a simple linear progression. We demonstrate that the higher orders form a logarithmic spiral, exhibit perfect 4-fold symmetry, and are governed by a scaled golden ratio constant (10x6φ). Most significantly, we show that the 200-order limit is not a physical detection threshold but a fundamental quantization boundary predicted by the UBP's OffBit state space.

This investigation employs the Three-Column Thinking methodology (Language, Mathematics, Script) inherent to the UBP 3.4 documentation [7] to ensure clarity, completeness, and verifiability.

\subsection{Three-Column Thinking: Geometric Optics}

\subsubsection{Column 1: Language (Narrative)}
Rainbows are formed by sunlight interacting with spherical water droplets. The angle of the rainbow depends on the number of internal reflections (p) the light undergoes and the refractive index (n) of water, which itself varies with wavelength (λ). The brightest part of the rainbow corresponds to the angle of minimum deviation, where a large number of light rays emerge at the same angle.

\subsubsection{Column 2: Mathematics (Formal)}
The angle of minimum deviation for a rainbow of order p is found by solving dD/di = 0, where D is the deviation angle and i is the incident angle. This yields a formula for the optimal incident angle:

\begin{equation}
\sin^2(i_{opt}) = \frac{(p+1)^2 - n^2}{p(p+2)}
\end{equation}

From this, the deviation angle D and the observable angle θ can be calculated:

\begin{equation}
D = 2i_{opt} - 2(p+1)r_{opt} + p\pi
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\theta = f(D, p) \pmod{360^\circ}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Column 3: Script (Executable)}
The mathematical formulas were implemented in Python to calculate the angles for all 200 orders across the visible spectrum (400-700 nm).

\begin{verbatim}
import numpy as np

def rainbow_angle_order_p(wavelength_nm, p):
    n = refractive_index_water(wavelength_nm)
    discriminant = ((p+1)**2 - n**2) / (p * (p+2))
    if not (0 <= discriminant <= 1.0):
        return None, False
    
    sin_i = np.sqrt(discriminant)
    i = np.arcsin(sin_i)
    r = np.arcsin(sin_i / n)
    
    D = 2*i - 2*(p+1)*r + p*np.pi
    
    if p == 1:
        theta = np.pi - D
    elif p == 2:
        theta = D - np.pi
    else:
        theta = np.abs(D - p*np.pi)
        
    return np.degrees(theta) % 360, True
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Three-Column Thinking: NRCI Coherence}

\subsubsection{Column 1: Language (Narrative)}
The visibility of higher-order rainbows is limited by the loss of coherence and intensity with each internal reflection. The Non-Random Coherence Index (NRCI) is a UBP metric that quantifies the degree of non-randomness in a system. We model the decay of NRCI to predict the theoretical limit of rainbow observability.

\subsubsection{Column 2: Mathematics (Formal)}
The NRCI decay is modeled as a product of intensity loss (Fresnel reflectance R), phase decoherence, and polarization mixing:

\begin{equation}
\text{NRCI}(p) = \text{NRCI}_0 \times R^{p-1} \times e^{-p/L_{coh}} \times e^{-p/L_{pol}}
\end{equation}

Where $L_{coh}$ and $L_{pol}$ are characteristic coherence lengths.

\subsubsection{Column 3: Script (Executable)}

\begin{verbatim}
def calculate_nrci_order_p(p, R=0.96, L_coh=250, L_pol=500):
    nrci_initial = 0.999997
    intensity_factor = R ** (p - 1)
    phase_decoherence = np.exp(-p / L_coh)
    polarization_factor = np.exp(-p / L_pol)
    nrci = nrci_initial * intensity_factor * phase_decoherence * polarization_factor
    return nrci
\end{verbatim}
